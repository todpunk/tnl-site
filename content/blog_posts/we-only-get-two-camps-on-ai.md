By valid, we mean something that can actually have any input on the outcomes of the exploration happening, rather than just making noise to express a feeling.

## The Anti-AI People
The biggest problem with anti-AI is what I will call the Loser Principle. We see this principle in lots of people doing things like "I don't like Amazon, so everyone should boycott them and just go without." It's ok to have valid criticism of anything, including AI. I have them in spades, but I can't be anti-AI, because I can't follow the Loser Principle. The Loser Principle is when someone takes a position where they're not going to get what they want, so they flip the game table, sabotage everyone else, cause a scene, whatever it takes to express their inability to present an actual case.

It's a [tantrum at the adults' table](/tnlblog/strong-feelings-disqualify-us-from-the-debate.html). A tantrum dressed up as a protest or some other "high morality" action is worse, because it's a tantrum that can't be honest.

## The First Camp: Skeptical Attempts

The actual anti-AI position that makes sense is [principled use](/tnlblog/ai-code-quality-does-not-matter-as-a-topic.html). Boycotts and refusals just hurt ourselves. They're an option to refuse to play the game that is hoping to be a table-flipping option, but can't because they can't lift the table. Principled use engages with the game but has a dialogue about the culture of how to properly wield the tool.

People who practice principled use do not have to agree either. One principled use may say AI visual art is fine with some classifiers. Another may say it's bad but writing is fine. The difference in positions causes good debate that we need in exploring these new tools. Some of these positions will eventually lose out entirely, but that's ok because losing the game is different than taking our ball and going home.

## The Second Camp: Hyped Reality

I personally think the anti-AI people are far worse than the AI hype bros, even though both are annoying. At least the hype bros are easily ignored and don't actively hate us for disagreeing. They're less likely to attack us personally, too.

That doesn't mean the AI hype bros have a valid position either. If Steve Yegge has AI psychosis, it's sad because he's more informed than we would expect such psychosis to happen under. When some MBA entrepreneur waste of oxygen has AI psychosis, it's expected. Both are a problem because they aren't playing the game, they're having a conversation about how good the game is while others are trying to play, and it's very distracting.

[Hype based on real outcomes](/tnlblog/it-works-is-never-enough.html) and becoming more informed through that excitement is the valid version. It _does_ things with the game. It _participates_ and pushes the boundaries. Maybe it's wrong, but wrong isn't invalid, it's willing to try something. If it's willing to try something known to not work, it might be stupid, but it isn't invalid.

We can easily see how much more disagreement there is to be had in this position, too. There's also overlap with the skeptical direction. The key here is that there is belief in the unknown being a huge upside. I don't share this position, but I would argue that it's entirely _valid_.

## This is not Moderation

Someone, an idiot of course, is going to just glaze over all this and assume this is just the middle of the road in two flavors. No, and I haven't said that. I did use the extremes that are stupid to point out the non-participants in the adventure game we find ourselves in. That's not to say that either group can't also just be extreme. A boycott is an extreme anti-AI position that only the most naive can take.

Other extreme anti-AI positions are more useful and actionable. Did model training steal from visual artists and authors of creative works? I would say no, but the sentiment that it's not exactly being fair to those artists is something I would agree on. Stealing is the wrong word. I can make my own Mona Lisa without crediting Leonardo Da Vinci, I just can't copy it. Plagiarism is a fuzzy line even when humans do it, but we know it's there.

The point there is that there are criticisms to make. LLMs are also really bad at a whole host of things. Pragmatics is a class of one of them in linguistics, but it's also really bad at "understanding" anything, because of course it is. When we use the tool thinking it "understands" mechanically, we run into major issues, because it can only _emulate_ understanding. The Chinese Room does not understand. It can not.

Extreme investment is also a valid position. If we believe it's going to be the entirety of the future, we need to demonstrate it. This is why hype people are so much more forgivable. It's non-obvious that they're patently wrong. Surely most of them will be, but not as much as the anti-AI people.

## We Need Each Other

The big takeaway isn't to be middle of the road, the goal here is to fight it out. The skeptics are finding problems the hype people will gloss over, partly because a bunch of them are actually just stupid and don't know how any of this works. Skeptics prove the incorrect bits. Hype people prove the possibilities and open new territory. This is a natural process.

If we don't have both, things go very wrong very quickly. AI Psychosis is real. It will get far worse than a few isolated news stories, too. It happens because despite the overlap between the two camps, they're pulling in a lot of very different directions on a lot of fronts. We need that balance, that [competitive cooperation](/tnlblog/competitive-cooperation-initial-thoughts.html), to make progress and build understanding of the tradeoffs. That's engineering.

The requirement is to play the game, not nope out of it, and not convincing others to nope out of it.
